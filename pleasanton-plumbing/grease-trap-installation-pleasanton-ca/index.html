    Frequently Asked Questions About Grease Trap Installation in Pleasanton

    Installed prices for small indoor hydromechanical traps typically fall between $1,500 and $5,000. Large outdoor gravity interceptors often run $8,000 to $30,000 or more once excavation and site restoration are counted. Brand is a small part of it. The written estimate holds unless the dig turns up a buried surprise.

    Plastic hydromechanical traps often last 20 years or more, while coated steel traps can rust out in 7 to 15 depending on how harsh the wastewater runs. A precast interceptor can last a very long time if it is pumped on schedule and the coating holds. Cleaning habits matter most. A trap that is scraped with a metal tool every week or left full of grease for months ages much faster than one that gets regular service.

    Two warranties apply here. Manufacturer coverage on the unit varies by brand and material, and plastic bodies usually get the longer terms. We cover our workmanship, meaning leaks at our joints and settling of a tank we set, and we give you the terms in writing before we start.

    On a commercial kitchen, no. Nearly every jurisdiction requires a licensed plumber to pull the permit and do the work. For a home kitchen you might be allowed to, as long as the flow control and venting are done correctly. Ask the building department first.

    Almost always, yes. You will usually need a plumbing permit plus a sign-off from the sewer authority's pretreatment program. We pull the permit, submit the sizing calculation and schedule the rough and final inspections. Expect an inspector on site.

    As close to the grease-producing fixtures as the space allows, so grease does not cool and coat the pipe before it reaches the trap. Indoor units need room above for the lid to come off and for someone to kneel and clean. Never under a cook line. Outdoor tanks should sit where the hauler can reach them and nothing gets parked on the lids.

    The common rule is to clean before grease and solids fill 25 percent of the trap's depth. For a small indoor trap in a busy kitchen, that can be weekly. Outdoor tanks go longer. A gravity interceptor typically gets pumped monthly to quarterly, and some sewer programs set the schedule for you.
