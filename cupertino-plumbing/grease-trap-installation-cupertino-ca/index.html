    Frequently Asked Questions About Grease Trap Installation in Cupertino

    Small under-sink hydromechanical traps usually land between $1,000 and $3,000 installed, and buried interceptors with excavation commonly run $5,000 to $20,000 or more. Most of the difference is labor, mainly digging and concrete repair. Permits add a little. We quote in writing after a site visit, with the trap, the labor and the permit fees listed as separate lines.

    Zurn and Watts make up a lot of our indoor installs, with Schier and Endura when a plastic body makes more sense. For outdoor work we set precast concrete tanks from nearby casting yards, or polyethylene interceptors when weight or access is a problem. Any certified brand works. We skip uncertified units, since inspectors turn them down.

    Size comes from flow, and flow comes from your fixtures. We add up the drain rate of each sink compartment, floor sink and dish machine, then apply the code's drain-down time to get gallons per minute. Gravity tanks use volume instead. Outdoor tanks get sized by the sewer authority's formula, which we run for you.

    The common rule is to clean before grease and solids fill 25 percent of the trap's depth. Busy kitchens with small indoor traps often hit that mark every week. Outdoor tanks go longer. Many gravity interceptors are pumped every 30 to 90 days, and your local FOG program may set a maximum interval no matter what the trap looks like.

    As close to the grease-producing fixtures as the space allows, so grease does not cool and coat the pipe before it reaches the trap. Indoor units need room above for the lid to come off and for someone to kneel and clean. Never under a cook line. Outdoor tanks should sit where the hauler can reach them and nothing gets parked on the lids.

    On a commercial kitchen, no. Nearly every jurisdiction requires a licensed plumber to pull the permit and do the work. For a home kitchen you might be allowed to, as long as the flow control and venting are done correctly. Ask the building department first.

    Two warranties apply here. Manufacturer coverage on the unit varies by brand and material, and plastic bodies usually get the longer terms. We cover our workmanship, meaning leaks at our joints and settling of a tank we set, and we give you the terms in writing before we start.

    Plastic hydromechanical traps often last 20 years or more, while coated steel traps can rust out in 7 to 15 depending on how harsh the wastewater runs. Concrete tanks can last decades, though hydrogen sulfide gas slowly eats the concrete above the water line. Cleaning habits matter most. Rough cleaning and long gaps between pump-outs shorten the life of every type.
