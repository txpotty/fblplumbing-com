    Hydro Jetting vs. Snaking: Which Drain Cleaning Method Do You Need?

    By Aaron Mitchell · August 22, 2026 · 6 min read

    Know the Clog First

    Not every clog is the same, and the tool should match what is actually in the pipe. A hair clog in a shower and a kitchen line coated with grease are two different problems. Clogs fall into two groups, a single plug at one spot or buildup along the whole pipe wall, and figuring out which one you have is the first thing a good tech does. Usually that means a few questions.

    Scale is the slow version of buildup. Hard water leaves mineral deposits inside drain pipe, especially old cast iron, that narrow the opening year by year. A 4-inch cast iron line with heavy scale might have the flow area of a 2-inch pipe, which is why it clogs again a week after a cable goes through it. Cabling cannot scrape that off.

    Homes on hard water see scale on fixtures and water heaters, while very soft water can be slightly corrosive to older copper pipe.

    How a Drain Snake Works

    A snake, or drain auger, is a coiled steel cable spun by a hand crank or a motor. On the end is a cutter or a corkscrew spring that drills into the blockage and breaks it apart or grabs it. Hand augers handle sinks and tubs, a closet auger handles toilets, a small drum machine handles laundry and kitchen lines, and a sectional machine with a 3/4-inch cable reaches a main line out to 100 feet or more. It is fast and cheap.

    Snaking is the right first call for a single hard blockage. A toilet stopped by a toy or a main plugged by roots at one joint both clear well with a cable. The weakness is that a cable opens a hole roughly the size of its cutter, so grease and scale on the rest of the pipe wall stay right where they were. A clog that returns within weeks is the giveaway.

    Cable size matters as much as the machine. A 1/4-inch hand cable is right for a bathroom sink but useless on a 4-inch main. Running too big a cable through a small trap can crack it, and running a small cable through a main just pokes a hole through a root mass that grows back within weeks. Matching the two takes experience.

    How Hydro Jetting Works

    A jetter pushes water through a hose at roughly 3,000 to 4,000 psi for residential lines. Its nozzle fires one jet forward to break through and a ring of rear jets that drive the hose and scrub the pipe. As the tech reels the hose back, the rear jets strip grease, scale, sludge and roots off the pipe all the way around, and the debris flushes downstream to the sewer. What is left is a nearly clean pipe wall.

    Jetting shines on greasy kitchen lines and scaled cast iron. Commercial kitchens jet their lines regularly for the same reason. It also clears sand and silt that a cable just pushes around, and a jetted line usually stays clear far longer than a cabled one, often years instead of months on a grease problem. It costs more per visit.

    Jetting needs a way in. An exterior cleanout is the best access point, since it keeps the mess out of the house. Homes without one may need a cleanout installed first, usually a quick job with a fitting and a riser at the foundation, and that new access point pays for itself the next time the line needs work. Pulling a toilet works in a pinch.

    Choosing Between Them

    Jetting is not safe for every pipe. Badly rotted cast iron or broken clay can come apart under high pressure. That is why a good tech runs a camera first on an older line, both to see what the clog is and to check whether the pipe can take a jetter without making the problem worse. Skip that step and a cleaning can turn into a dig.

    Here is the rough rule we use. One hard plug in pipe that is in decent shape gets snaked. A kitchen line that clogs every few months or a main coated with grease or scale gets a camera and then a jetter, and that combination usually stops the repeat calls for a long time. Cost plays into it too.

    When to Call a Professional

    Rental drum machines are where a lot of homeowner drain jobs go sideways. A kinked cable can lodge in the pipe or whip back at the operator. If a plunger and a small hand auger have not cleared it, or more than one drain is backing up, call FBL Plumbing at (669) 319-2034 and describe which fixtures are affected, because that tells the tech which line to start on and which tools to bring. Mention when the last cleaning was.
