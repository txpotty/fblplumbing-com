    Why a Sewer Camera Inspection Belongs on Every Northern California Home Inspection

    By Andrew Blake · August 13, 2026 · 5 min read

    What a Home Inspection Does Not Cover

    A general home inspector runs water in the sinks, flushes toilets and checks for leaks under cabinets. What they usually do not do is look inside the main sewer line. That line runs from the house to the city main or the septic tank, often 40 to 100 feet underground, and if it is broken the repair can cost more than a new roof, which lands on the new owner the day the deed records. The scope takes about an hour.

    Running water for ten minutes proves very little. A line with a crack or a root mat can still drain a sink just fine. Problems show up once a family of four starts doing laundry and taking showers every day, usually in the first few months after move-in. A house that sat empty is the hardest to judge.

    Common Sewer Line Problems in Northern California Homes

    Expansive clay soils are common across Northern California, and seasonal swelling and shrinking can crack slabs and buried sewer laterals.

    Roots are the most common thing we find on a pre-purchase scope. They enter at joints in clay and cast iron and grow into mats that catch paper. Bellies, sections that have sagged and hold water, are next, followed by offset joints where one piece of pipe has shifted out of line with the next one. Each one shows clearly on video.

    Grease and scale show up on older lines as well. A thick coat narrows the pipe and catches paper at every joint. A seller who had the line cleaned the week before listing can make a bad pipe look better on paper, so ask when the last cleaning was and read the video for fresh cutter marks on the walls. Cutter marks are easy to spot on camera.

    Copper became standard in many mid-century homes here, and decades later pinhole leaks and worn fittings are a common repair call.

    The age of the house tells you what pipe to expect. Homes built before the 1980s often have cast iron or clay laterals, and some have Orangeburg. Cast iron in its fifties and sixties commonly shows heavy scale or channel rot, where the floor of the pipe has worn through, while PVC from newer builds usually looks clean unless it was bedded poorly. Request records of earlier repairs from the seller.

    What Skipping It Can Cost

    Sewer line replacement is one of the most expensive repairs a house can need, right alongside a foundation or a roof. A line that runs under a driveway or a big tree costs more again. Trenchless lining or bursting often brings the price down and saves the yard, but nobody wants to learn about a collapsed lateral two weeks after closing, when the seller is no longer part of the conversation. Before closing, a bad line is something you can negotiate.

    A camera report gives you options. You can ask the seller to repair the line or credit the cost at closing. Homeowner policies generally exclude gradual deterioration and root damage to a sewer line, so the cost lands on the buyer if nobody looks before the papers are signed. Some buyers decide to walk.

    Septic homes need an extra look. A scope down the outlet line shows the tank inlet and the condition of the baffle. A full septic inspection with a pump-out is a separate service, but the camera pass tells you whether the pipe between house and tank is sound before you spend money on the tank itself. Rural listings deserve both.

    When to Call a Professional

    Book the scope during your inspection window, ideally in the first few days so there is time to get a repair quote if the video shows trouble. Access is usually through an outdoor cleanout, or through a toilet flange when the house has none. FBL Plumbing records the full run, marks the footage and depth of any defect with a locator, and sends you the video with a written summary you can hand to the seller, and the scheduling line is (669) 319-2034. Bring your agent in early.

    What a Good Camera Inspection Includes

    Look for a report with video, not a pass or fail checkbox. The camera should travel the whole line, cleanout to main or tank. Footage markers and a locator reading at any problem spot let a second plumber or the seller's contractor quote the same repair without rescoping, which saves days during a tight closing schedule. Keep the file after you move in.

    Owners of older homes can use a scope too. A baseline scope every few years catches roots while they are still small. If the house has had two or more main line backups in a year, the line almost always has a defect worth seeing on video, and cleaning it again without looking just resets the clock. Camera first, then decide.
