    No permit is needed just to have a trap pumped. The records are your responsibility. Installing a new trap or interceptor, or changing the size of one, usually needs a plumbing permit and sometimes a pretreatment program review before the work starts.

    Skimming off the top layer yourself is usually allowed. The disposal is the part that gets people fined. Pretreatment programs often require a licensed hauler's record for each pump-out, so a kitchen that handles its own cleaning may have no paperwork to show when the inspector asks.

    Slow sinks come first. Then gurgling floor drains and a sour smell near the dish area. By the time gray water is coming up through a floor sink, the trap is usually well past 25 percent full and grease is already heading down the line.

    Rules come from your sewer district or city pretreatment program. The 25 percent rule shows up in most of them.     FOG stands for fats, oils and grease. It pours as a liquid and sets up hard once it cools. Inside a sewer pipe it builds up in layers on the walls, traps food and wipes, and can shrink the opening to the size of a pencil.

    The terms get mixed up constantly. Most people use trap for the small indoor unit, usually under 100 gallons. Interceptor usually means the large tank in the ground outside, often 500 gallons and up, and codes size the two in different ways.
