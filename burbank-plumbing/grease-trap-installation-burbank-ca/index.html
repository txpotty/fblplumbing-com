    Frequently Asked Questions About Grease Trap Installation in Burbank

    Installed prices for small indoor hydromechanical traps typically fall between $1,500 and $5,000. Outdoor tanks cost more, mostly for digging and putting the site back. Brand is a small part of it. We give you a written estimate after we walk the site, and it does not change unless we hit something underground nobody could see.

    We start with a fixture list and the drain rate of each fixture. Each sink's volume and drain time give a flow rate, and the dish machine's spec sheet gives its flow. That gives the GPM figure. Indoor units match the GPM, and outdoor tanks get sized by volume and retention.

    You get the manufacturer's warranty on the unit and a written labor warranty from us on the installation. We register the unit with the maker so the factory warranty is on record. Keep your cleaning logs. Some manufacturers ask for proof of regular service before they honor a claim on a cracked body or a failed baffle.

    Pump-outs every couple of weeks on a trap that used to go two months is the clearest sign you have outgrown it or the baffles have failed. Grease odor in the dining room is another. Rust shows up too. We also look downstream, because grease in the outlet pipe means the trap is letting it through.

    Half a day for most indoor units. We set it, connect it, add the flow control and test it with water. Outdoor tanks need two to five days, with an inspection before backfill. Permits add lead time.

    Coverage comes from two places. The manufacturer warrants the trap body and lid, and the term depends on the brand and whether it is steel or plastic. Our written labor warranty covers the joints we made and how we set the unit. Damage from skipped cleanings falls outside it.

    Indoor hydromechanical traps and outdoor gravity interceptors cover nearly every job we do. Automatic grease removal units are a pricier option for kitchens that fry all day. Most cafes go hydromechanical. High-volume kitchens usually get an outdoor gravity tank, especially if the sewer authority asks for one.

    In everyday talk the two names get swapped. The trade usually calls the small indoor unit a trap and the big outdoor tank an interceptor. Codes vary on the wording. Some plumbing codes call both of them interceptors and separate them as hydromechanical and gravity, so we use whatever terms your permit office uses.
