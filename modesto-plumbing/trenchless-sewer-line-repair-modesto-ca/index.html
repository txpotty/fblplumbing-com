    Root Intrusion Repair in Modesto, CA

    Roots enter at the joints, where clay bells or cast iron hubs have a small gap or a failed gasket. Once in, they fill the pipe. We cut them out with a mechanical root cutter or a hydro jet, then line the pipe so those joints are sealed for good and the line stops needing a cleaning every few months. The roots cannot get back in.

    Pipe Bursting Sewer Line Replacements in Modesto

    A bursting head is a steel cone, slightly wider than the old pipe, pulled through the line by a hydraulic unit or a winch cable. As it moves, it splits the old clay or cast iron and shoves the pieces into the surrounding soil while a string of new HDPE pipe follows right behind it. Fused joints, no gaps. This is the fix for collapsed pipe.

    Cured-In-Place Pipe Relining in Modesto, CA

    CIPP stands for cured-in-place pipe. The crew saturates a felt tube with two-part epoxy on a roller table at the truck, invert it into the cleaned line with air pressure, and hold it against the old pipe wall until the resin hardens. Cure time runs a few hours. The finished liner seals cracks and leaking joints in the original pipe.

    Orangeburg and Clay Pipe Replacement in Modesto

    Orangeburg is bituminous fiber pipe that was laid in houses mostly from the late 1940s into the early 1970s. Over time it softens, goes oval and blisters on the inside, so a liner has nothing round to bond to and bursting is usually the better fix. Clay is different. Vitrified clay that still holds shape lines well.

    Perma-Liner and NuFlow Trenchless Systems in Modesto

    Perma-Liner and NuFlow both make lining systems we use, each with its own resin chemistry and installation equipment. Both are built to ASTM standards for cured-in-place pipe. The brand matters less than the prep, because a liner bonded to a dirty, scaly pipe wall will not hold the way the manufacturer rates it. So the line gets jetted or descaled to bare wall before any resin goes in.

    Cast Iron Sewer Line Repair in Modesto, CA

    Cast iron rusts from the inside out, and the bottom of the pipe goes first. Plumbers call that channel rot, a groove that eats through the bottom. We descale the pipe with chain knockers or carbide cutters spinning on a flexible shaft, which brings the wall back to bare metal so a liner can bond to it. Under a slab, lining beats breaking up the floor in two rooms.
